Where your water comes from changes the emergency

Indian Hills is an unincorporated community of about 1,474 residents (2020 census) in Jefferson County. Some homes take water from the Indian Hills Water District, a special district with its office on Parmalee Gulch Road. Other homes sit outside it and run on a private well with a septic system, according to Jefferson County Public Health.

That split matters the moment something breaks. A district customer with a burst line has one job, find and stop the leak inside the house. A well owner also has a pump, a pressure tank and a power circuit feeding both, so a wet utility room can mean a shorted pump as well as soaked drywall.

Supply interruptions are part of local life

In January 2024 the district found a main leak on a very steep hillside, and repairs took days. Water was trucked in, and a boil advisory followed. If your home was affected, or the next failure hits you, you may be cleaning and drying a structure while the tap runs dry or the water is not cleared to drink.

Drying itself needs very little supply, but cleanup and rinsing do. Ask the district what its current status is before you plan a wash-down, and keep stored water for that purpose. If you are on a well, Jefferson County Public Health recommends yearly testing, and offers free bacteria and nitrate kits while supplies last.

When water is spreading in Indian Hills

Water in a house at 7,200 feet behaves differently from water in a flat suburb. It follows floor slope into crawl spaces, runs down framing, and settles in the lowest finished room, often a walkout basement. Cold nights can also split a supply line in an unheated crawl space, and the damage may run unnoticed for a day.

Before we arrive, if water is still spreading

Stop the source

Find the shutoff on the line that is leaking and close it. On a well system, switch off the pump breaker only if the panel is dry and you can reach it safely.

Power only if safe and dry

Leave wet outlets, basement panels and any standing water alone. If you cannot tell, stay out and call the electric utility.

Lift what you can

Move rugs, electronics, papers and photos to dry shelves. Leave heavy furniture where it stands.

Keep people and pets out

Water from a septic backflow, a washing machine or a flooded yard can carry contamination. Keep children and animals away and call the line.

Stay out if you see any of these

  • Water touching outlets, a panel, a pressure tank wiring box or a running pump. Do not step in. Call the electric utility or 911 before anything else.
  • A gas smell. Leave the house at once and call 911 from outside.
  • Sewage or septic backflow in a drain, shower or yard. Treat it as contaminated, keep everyone out, and ask Jefferson County Public Health about septic questions.
  • A sagging ceiling holding water. Do not stand under it.

Terrain, access and what it means for the first hour of work

Roads here wind and climb, and many homes sit on steep, narrow driveways. The fire district alone covers ground from 5,845 to 8,225 feet. When you call, tell us your road, any gate code, whether the driveway is gravel or paved, and whether snow or ice is on it, so equipment can be planned before the truck leaves. We approach from CO-74 or US-285, depending on where you are.

Slope also moves water indoors. A hillside lot can push runoff against a foundation wall during a heavy rain or fast melt, so we check below-grade rooms for seepage and run a water mitigation plan that dries framing and subfloors, not just visible carpet.

Parmalee Gulch, Mount Falcon and nearby ground

Parmalee Gulch Road is the main spine for homes in ZIP 80454, and the fire station sits on it at number 4476. About 36 percent of the fire district is public land, including parts of Mount Falcon Park and Parmalee Gulch Park, so some properties back onto open ground where runoff and snowmelt can reach the lot. The Mount Falcon West Trailhead is on Mount Falcon Road, a useful landmark when you tell us where you are.

Turkey Creek drains this side of the foothills too. A county-commissioned study recorded a peak flow of 1,200 cubic feet per second on August 24, 1946. If creek water or storm runoff ever gets into your house, that is a flood damage restoration job, and flood damage is usually excluded from standard homeowners policies, so check your policy and ask your insurer.

My neighbors have no water after a district main leak. Can drying still happen?

Often yes. Extraction and drying use equipment, not tap water, though wash-down and cleaning do need water. Ask the Indian Hills Water District for its current status, and keep stored water for cleanup.

I have a well. What should I check after a flood or pipe burst?

Switch off the pump only if the panel is dry, then keep people from drinking from the system until you have tested it. Jefferson County Public Health recommends yearly well testing offers free bacteria and nitrate kits while supplies last, and also regulates septic systems in unincorporated areas.
