How storms put water inside a foothill house

The Mile High Flood District, in its history of the Bear Creek basin, notes that most floods there have been summer thunderstorm floods. The same storms cause plenty of damage long before a creek rises. A cell can sit over one slope, drop rain at a rate gutters cannot carry and push it sideways at siding and windows.

Common ways storm water gets in:

  • Wind-driven rain. Water is forced under siding, around window frames and through gaps in deck-to-wall flashing that stay dry in ordinary weather.
  • Hail. Impact cracks shingles, dents flashing and can crack skylights, so water follows the next rain.
  • Clogged gutters and downspouts. Overflow runs down the wall and soaks the rim joist and basement wall.
  • Runoff against the foundation. Water pools where the ground slopes toward the house, then seeps through the wall or a window well.
  • Debris-blocked drains. Needles, hail and sediment close off a window well or area drain.

EPA homeowner guidance on moisture says the ground should slope away from the house, and that missing gutters or water flowing toward the walls can cause basement water. After a storm, that is worth checking from the outside before the next one.

Storm water or flood water: where the line falls

The cause decides the process. Rain that enters through the envelope of the house, such as a roof or a window, is usually treated as clean or lightly dirty water and dried in place. Water that rose from outside and moved across the ground into the house picks up soil and contamination, and it is handled as Category 3 under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard.

Storm water safety

  • Stay off any room where water is near outlets, a panel or a furnace. Switch off power to the area only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water.
  • Do not walk through water that came across the yard. Treat it as contaminated.
  • Keep clear of ceilings that bulge or sag, and avoid rooms under a damaged roof until it is checked.
  • Never drive into water across a road. Roads in canyons can close during storms.
  • Photograph the damage and the outside of the house before you tarp, sweep or move anything.
  • Use buckets for drips and plastic for furniture, and leave drying decisions for the crew.

Storm damage and insurance

Rain entering through a storm-damaged roof or window is often covered under a homeowners policy, since the damage was sudden. Hail damage may fall under a separate deductible, so ask your insurer. Flood damage from runoff or a creek is usually excluded and needs a separate flood policy. Seepage over time is often treated as a maintenance issue. Check your policy, call your insurer promptly, and keep photos of both the exterior and the interior. We cannot promise any approval.

Mistakes after a storm

  • Pumping a basement down all at once. Saturated soil can push on a wall that is no longer balanced by water inside.
  • Leaving wet carpet down because the sun came out.
  • Tarping over wet shingles without photographs.
  • Skipping the outside check of gutters and grading, so the next storm repeats the problem.

Frequently asked questions

Is emergency storm water damage cleanup available in Evergreen, CO?

Yes. The line is answered 24/7, including nights during storm season. Same-day response is offered when available, and the dispatcher asks about road conditions and utilities before a crew is sent.

How do I tell storm water from flood water?

Look at the path. Water that came through the roof, a window or a wall is storm intrusion. Water that crossed the ground or rose from a creek is a flood loss and carries contamination.

Does hail damage the inside of the house?

Not directly, but broken shingles and cracked skylights let rain in during the next storm. A ceiling stain after hail is a reason to check the attic.

Can window leaks after wind-driven rain be dried in place?

Often yes. The crew checks the wall cavity with a moisture meter and removes only what cannot dry.

Should I wait for the insurance adjuster before cleaning?

Photograph first and call your insurer promptly, but do not leave wet materials sitting. Ask your insurer what it needs, since prompt drying limits further damage.
